# NOMENCLATURE ENTRY 5
## It Resembled a Horse from One Direction.

**Organisms observed:** Several  
**Proposed relationships:** Numerous  
**Confirmed relationships:** Considerably fewer  
**Primary naming method:** Partial resemblance  
**Required viewing angle:** Specific

Humans frequently name an unfamiliar organism after a familiar organism.

This would be a useful preliminary classification method if the unfamiliar organism consistently resembled the familiar one.

It does not.

The resemblance may involve one feature.

It may depend upon distance.

It may depend upon lighting.

It may exist only when the organism is viewed from the side.

In several cases, it appears to have been established by a human who had seen a different animal recently and remained emotionally committed to the comparison.

Once the resemblance has been announced, the new organism may inherit the familiar animal’s name regardless of anatomy, ancestry, habitat, behavior, or the objections of every taxonomist who encounters it afterward.

This produces biological relationships which do not exist.

It also preserves the precise moment in which one human looked at something unprecedented and attempted to explain it using the animals already available.

### The Horse

The seahorse is a fish.

This fact should be established immediately because the human name does not assist with it.

A seahorse possesses gills, fins, a prehensile tail, bony external plates, independently moving eyes, and a reproductive arrangement in which the male carries the developing young.

It does not possess hooves.

It does not gallop.

It cannot pull agricultural equipment.

Its suitability for riding is negligible.

The resemblance to a horse is concentrated almost entirely in the head and neck.

Even there, the comparison requires generosity.

Viewed in profile, the head projects forward at an angle which reminded some human observer of a horse.

Viewed from another direction, it resembles a seahorse.

The human nevertheless selected *horse* as the organism’s defining feature and added *sea* to explain why the horse was underwater.

This naming structure suggests that humans first identify what an organism resembles and then treat the habitat as a modification.

A seahorse is therefore not a fish whose head resembles that of a horse.

It is a horse altered by the sea.

I attempted to locate the point at which the terrestrial horse became aquatic, reduced in size, acquired gills, replaced its hindquarters with a coiled tail, and transferred gestation to the male.

There is no such point.

The organisms are not closely related.

The name records a visual comparison, not an evolutionary one.

This should be obvious.

It was not obvious before I began constructing the lineage chart.

### The Aquatic Exception

Earth’s oceans appear to weaken human naming discipline.

When an unfamiliar organism is encountered in water, humans frequently take the name of something recognizable and add either *sea* or *fish*.

This does not indicate that the organism is a fish.

Within common nomenclature, *fish* can mean:

- a fish;
- an organism living near fish;
- an organism with a fish-like outline;
- an organism which moves in water;
- an organism discovered while the human was already thinking about fish;
- or an organism for which the meeting had continued too long.

A starfish is not a fish.

It is an echinoderm.

A jellyfish is not a fish.

It is a cnidarian.

A cuttlefish is a mollusk.

A silverfish is an insect and does not live in the sea at all.

The word had escaped containment.

Scientists have attempted to repair portions of this system.

*Starfish* may be called *sea stars.*

*Jellyfish* may be shortened to *jellies.*

These revisions improve taxonomic clarity while making the organisms sound respectively like celestial objects and preserves.

The public continues using the earlier names.

They already know what a starfish is.

The starfish, having survived for hundreds of millions of years without being a star or a fish, appears capable of enduring the misunderstanding.

### The Star

The name *starfish* is at least visually defensible.

Most familiar examples possess arms radiating from a central body.

The resulting shape resembles the diagram humans use to represent a star.

It does not greatly resemble an actual star.

Actual stars are enormous spheres of plasma undergoing nuclear fusion.

The common five-pointed star symbol resembles neither their physical structure nor their visible shape at close range.

The starfish therefore resembles the human drawing of something which does not resemble the thing the drawing represents.

This resemblance was sufficient.

The name was assigned.

The paperwork followed.

Human comparative naming sometimes operates across several layers of symbolic abstraction without acknowledging that it has left the original object behind.

The starfish does not look like a star.

It looks like what humans decided stars look like when drawing them for children.

This is apparently close enough for biology.

### The Jelly

The jellyfish presents a different problem.

The organism does resemble jelly.

It is translucent, soft, unstable when unsupported, and difficult to handle with dignity.

This portion of the name is unusually successful.

The word *fish* was then added for reasons which appear to be primarily aquatic.

The resulting name describes the texture accurately and the organism incorrectly.

This is still considered adequate.

Humans have since attempted to replace *jellyfish* with *sea jelly*.

This removes the false animal relationship but introduces the possibility of edible ocean preserves.

I asked whether the humans had considered creating an entirely new word.

They had.

They preferred not to use it.

The familiar error was easier to carry.

### The Pig from Guinea

The guinea pig is neither a pig nor from Guinea.

This fact is presented to human children with the satisfaction normally reserved for a riddle.

The animal is a rodent originating in South America.

Its relationship to pigs is limited.

It possesses a compact body, makes vocal sounds, eats enthusiastically, and was apparently judged pig-like by someone operating under flexible standards.

The geographical portion is less helpful.

Multiple explanations have been proposed for how *Guinea* entered the name. None establish the animal as Guinean.

Thus, the guinea pig was assigned both the wrong organism and the wrong continent.

The name persists.

This is an impressive degree of inaccuracy for two words.

Humans do not generally find the name confusing because they learn the entire phrase as a single unit.

A guinea pig is a guinea pig.

The individual meanings of *Guinea* and *pig* are suspended during use.

This demonstrates that a familiar compound name can remain functional even after every component has resigned from factual service.

The animal itself seems unconcerned.

It has received vegetables.

### The Prairie Dog

The prairie dog is also a rodent.

It does live on prairies.

This gives the name an initial accuracy rate of fifty percent.

The dog portion refers primarily to its alarm call, which humans thought resembled barking.

The organism was therefore named after the sound of an unrelated animal.

This expands the comparative system.

An unfamiliar creature may inherit another creature’s name because it resembles the creature visually.

It may also inherit the name because it sounds like the creature.

The resemblance need not involve the whole organism.

A noise is sufficient.

Under this system, a human hearing one species from behind a rock may establish a relationship that remains in the language long after the rock has been removed.

Prairie dogs live in complex social colonies, maintain burrow systems, communicate danger, and alter the ecosystems around them.

They are not dogs.

They once made a noise in the presence of someone who knew what a dog sounded like.

The observer disappeared.

The reaction survived.

### The Bear Problem

Humans also add *bear* to organisms that are not bears.

The koala is frequently called a koala bear.

It is a marsupial.

The water bear is a tardigrade.

It is microscopic.

The woolly bear is a caterpillar.

It will eventually become a moth.

None are bears.

The application of *bear* appears to require some combination of rounded shape, dense covering, compact movement, or a general impression that the organism might be more understandable if it were placed near a bear linguistically.

The resemblance may be affectionate.

It may be visual.

It may be almost entirely atmospheric.

Taxonomy is not consulted.

The bear has become less an animal than a naming resource.

Humans borrow it whenever an unfamiliar organism produces a sufficiently bear-adjacent feeling.

### Questionable Kinship

I initially treated these names as evidence of biological relationships.

This was a mistake.

I constructed a provisional terrestrial lineage in which horses produced seahorses, pigs migrated through Guinea to South America, dogs established subterranean prairie settlements, stars entered the ocean, and bears diversified into marsupials, caterpillars, and microscopic extremophiles.

The model was internally ambitious.

It was externally useless.

A human reviewed it and laughed for an extended period.

The human then attempted to explain common names.

Common names, I was told, are not designed to encode precise evolutionary information.

They develop through resemblance, habit, regional usage, translation, error, metaphor, trade, folklore, and whichever feature a human noticed first.

This explanation accounted for the evidence.

It did not improve the database.

In my own language, the designation of an organism may encode ancestry, environmental role, biochemical characteristics, developmental pattern, and the degree of confidence associated with the classification.

A competent speaker can extract substantial information from the name alone.

Human common names may encode that the organism looked slightly like a horse from the left.

I considered this primitive.

I have revised that assessment.

It is primitive.

It is also something else.

### The Familiar Carried into the Unfamiliar

Humans do not encounter new life without context.

When they see an organism for the first time, they search the existing world for a point of contact.

What does it resemble?

What sound does it make?

How does it move?

What texture does it suggest?

What known creature has already prepared the mind to contain this one?

The comparison may be scientifically useless.

It may nevertheless reveal the encounter with unusual precision.

*Seahorse* preserves the instant in which a human recognized a familiar profile inside an unfamiliar body.

*Prairie dog* preserves the instant in which a sound crossed the space between species and was understood through another sound.

*Jellyfish* preserves texture.

*Starfish* preserves shape.

*Guinea pig* preserves a historical confusion so complete that its exact route can no longer be reconstructed with confidence.

These names do not reliably describe the organisms.

They describe what happened inside humans when the organisms became known.

The animal contributed its body.

The human contributed a memory.

The name formed where they met.

### Field Test

I attempted to reproduce the method.

The selected organism was a giraffe.

A giraffe may be described as:

- a long-necked spotted horse;
- a terrestrial browsing crane;
- a vertical deer;
- or a tree-adjacent mammal assembled with insufficient concern for proportion.

All of these descriptions identify visible characteristics.

None should become the organism’s name.

I now understand that this objection would not necessarily have stopped the humans.

The success of a common name does not depend upon completeness.

It depends upon recognition.

A human who has never seen a seahorse can hear the name and prepare for something small, aquatic, and vaguely horse-shaped.

The preparation will be inaccurate.

It will also be better than nothing.

That appears to be the governing standard.

### Field Note

Human comparative names should not be used to infer ancestry.

They may, however, be used to infer attention.

The chosen comparison identifies the feature that crossed first into human awareness.

The seahorse’s head arrived before the rest of it.

The prairie dog’s voice arrived before its anatomy.

The starfish’s outline arrived before its phylum.

The jellyfish’s texture arrived before nearly everything else.

The name is therefore not a compressed specimen.

It is a compressed first impression.

This explains both its scientific limitations and its unusual durability.

Later observers may learn more about the organism.

They may dissect it, sequence it, classify it, revise its lineage, and document its ecological role.

But the first astonished comparison remains attached.

The organism acquires an official designation.

It continues carrying the moment someone pointed and said what it looked like.

### Final Observation

There is something unexpectedly gentle in this practice.

Human beings enter an unfamiliar world carrying a relatively small collection of familiar animals, objects, sounds, and shapes.

When they encounter something new, they do not leave it entirely outside that collection.

They make room by analogy.

The analogy may be poor.

The resulting kinship may be indefensible.

But for one brief instant, the unknown thing becomes less unknown.

It resembles a horse.

It sounds like a dog.

It has the shape humans have assigned to a star.

It moves like a bear, feels like jelly, or reminds someone of a pig for reasons later generations will be unable to determine.

The name is not only an error.

It is evidence of the bridge the human mind constructed during first contact.

A more advanced classification system would remove the false relationships.

It would also remove the encounter.

It would tell us what the organism is.

It would not tell us what the human reached for when they first saw it.

Human nomenclature retains both.

The seahorse remains a fish.

The horse remains on land.

They are unrelated except for a profile, an observer, and a word.

This was enough to join them permanently.

---

**GENERAL RULE:**

When humans encounter a life-form they do not recognize, they compare it to one they already love, fear, use, remember, or have recently seen.

Accuracy may follow.

The resemblance gets the name.
