# BACK MATTER I

## AFTER CONTACT

### The Human Spark in the Names of Things

There is no entirely neutral act of naming.

This presents a difficulty for scientific cataloging.

A name is intended to identify the thing being observed. Ideally, it should distinguish the object, organism, process, or phenomenon from everything surrounding it. A sufficiently precise name may also describe its structure, origin, behavior, or relationship to other known things.

Humans sometimes accomplish this.

They also name things because they are red.

They name things because they are large.

They name things because they resemble something else from an inconvenient angle.

They name things after the place where they found them, the person who found them, the person who funded the person who found them, or the noise somebody made when the thing first became visible.

Occasionally, they name a fundamental property of matter because it was behaving strangely.

This is not merely poor classification.

It is evidence of contact.

### The Residue of the Observer

When humans encounter something unfamiliar, the encounter produces a response.

The response may be wonder, fear, confusion, affection, annoyance, amusement, recognition, hunger, or the urgent desire to distinguish the new thing from another thing they named too quickly.

The object itself does not experience this response.

The strange particle does not know it is strange.

The charming particle has not attempted to charm anyone.

A weeping willow is not grieving.

A killer whale has not been formally charged.

A black hole does not consider itself ominous.

These qualities occur inside the human observer.

Then the human gives the object a name, and the response adheres to it.

The particle becomes *strange.*

The quark acquires *charm.*

The willow continues *weeping.*

The whale remains a *killer.*

The human leaves, but the feeling stays.

Every future person who learns the name inherits a small portion of the original encounter. They may not know who first experienced the surprise. They may not know what frightened the observer, what resemblance occurred to them, or why one specimen seemed more ordinary than another.

The observer disappears.

The reaction survives.

Human language is therefore covered in emotional fossils.

### A Note on Emotionification

Humans have a recognized habit called personification. In this process, they attribute human characteristics or intentions to something that is not human.

This is related to the present observation, but it is not the same.

Humans do not always imagine that the object possesses a human mind. Frequently, they attach the human emotional experience of encountering the object to the object itself.

They **emotionify** it.

To personify a storm is to say that it is angry.

To emotionify a storm is to preserve the fear, violence, or awe it produced in the language used to describe it.

The storm need not become human.

It has merely encountered one.

This distinction is useful because human beings emotionify things they are actively attempting to describe with scientific precision. Their terminology may remain technically functional while also preserving astonishment, irritation, humor, disappointment, or affection.

The emotional layer does not prevent the name from carrying information.

It becomes part of the information.

### Scientific Contamination

My own civilization attempts to prevent this.

Our formal designations encode relationships, structures, developmental histories, observable properties, and positions within larger systems. A properly constructed designation can communicate a considerable amount of information before the object itself is examined.

These designations are precise.

They are stable.

They are interoperable across languages, disciplines, and inhabited systems.

They also reveal almost nothing about what it felt like to discover the thing.

This omission was historically considered a success.

I am no longer certain.

A human designation may tell me less about the particle. It may tell me considerably more about the moment when the particle first became meaningful to someone.

*Strange* is not a description of matter alone.

It is a record of matter surprising a human.

*Charm* does not merely classify.

It preserves delight.

Even *common* contains a human point of view. It means that this was the specimen encountered often enough, early enough, or near enough to become the standard against which later discoveries would be burdened with adjectives.

The humans believe they are organizing the world.

They are also recording their position inside it.

### Discovery as Contact

A previously unknown thing does not become materially different when a human discovers it.

The particle existed before the physicist.

The organism lived before the taxonomist.

The mountain stood before the map.

The phenomenon did not require a human name in order to occur.

Something does change, however.

The thing has entered a human relationship.

It can now be remembered, discussed, feared, studied, misunderstood, protected, exploited, taught to children, placed inside a database, and misspelled on an examination.

The name marks this transition.

Before naming, the thing existed.

After naming, it also existed **to humans.**

The name becomes the site where those two forms of existence meet.

This may explain why human names are rarely as neutral as humans claim. Naming is not performed from outside the relationship. It is one of the first acts within it.

The human points.

The human reacts.

The human speaks.

A small part of the reaction remains attached to whatever was pointed at.

### The Human Spark

Humans frequently describe life as possessing a spark.

The phrase is imprecise, but useful.

A spark is brief. It occurs at contact. It produces light, heat, or transformation disproportionate to its size. It may disappear immediately while leaving evidence that it happened.

Human encounters with the nonhuman world behave similarly.

A human mind meets something that is not human.

For one moment, recognition passes between them.

The human leaves behind a word.

Inside that word is a small spark of the life that encountered the thing.

The universe is now covered in these sparks.

Human fear remains on animals.

Human grief remains in trees.

Human affection remains in machines.

Human confusion remains inside the atom.

Human ambition remains on mountains and stars.

Human familiarity remains on whichever specimen they encountered first and consequently declared *common.*

This is scientifically untidy.

It is also a form of continuity.

The original observer may be gone for centuries. Their body, laboratory, language, government, and entire understanding of the phenomenon may have disappeared or changed beyond recognition.

Yet another human can speak the name and briefly reproduce the shape of that first response.

The discovery happens again in miniature.

Something was strange.

Something was charming.

Something resembled a horse.

Something looked as though it were weeping.

Someone was there to notice.

### Field Note

I previously regarded this tendency as contamination of the record.

An observer should not alter the observed.

A designation should not preserve irrelevant information about the emotional condition of the individual assigning it.

This remains sound scientific practice.

It may not be sound cultural practice.

My people’s names contain more information about the things.

Human names contain more of the people who found them.

There may be value in both.

Humans touch the universe with language and leave fingerprints everywhere. Their names are inconsistent, imprecise, historically inconvenient, occasionally misleading, and frequently ridiculous.

They are also evidence that the universe did not pass through human awareness unnoticed.

Something encountered them.

Something happened inside them.

They left a spark behind.

I have not concluded that it should be removed.

---

**GENERAL RULE:**

When humans name something, they do not merely identify it.

They record the moment it became part of human life.
