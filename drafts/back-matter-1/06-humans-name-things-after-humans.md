# NOMENCLATURE ENTRY 6
## Humans Name Things After Humans.

**Naming subjects observed:** Plants, buildings, streets, institutions, scientific principles  
**Status of named human:** Frequently deceased  
**Effect upon human:** Symbolic continuation  
**Effect upon object:** Additional administrative burden  
**Persistence mechanism:** Repetition

Humans name things after other humans.

They name scientific principles after humans.

They name plants after humans.

They name buildings, roads, bridges, airports, schools, libraries, hospitals, parks, scholarships, storms, ships, mountains, awards, and rooms after humans.

This practice frequently occurs after the named human has died.

Death appears to improve eligibility considerably.

A living human may possess a name.

A dead human may become infrastructure.

### The Proposed Function

At first, I assumed eponymous naming was a record-management system.

A building bearing a person’s name would presumably indicate that the person designed it, constructed it, lived there, performed important work inside it, or possessed some other direct relationship to the structure.

This is not required.

The person may have donated money.

The person may have governed the region.

The person may have attended the institution.

The person may have died before construction began.

The person may have been selected by a committee seeking a name with sufficient dignity to place above an entrance.

The relationship between human and object varies considerably.

The name remains.

A student may therefore spend four years entering a building named after someone they never met, walking along a road named after someone they have never studied, and sitting in a room named after someone whose principal contribution to the room was being deceased when the plaque was ordered.

The student gradually stops perceiving these as human names.

They become locations.

*Einstein* becomes a hall.

*Washington* becomes an avenue.

*Carnegie* becomes a library.

The human being is compressed into navigation.

This appeared to me an inefficient memorial system.

I had not yet encountered the begonia.

### Einstein’s Begonia

Albert Einstein kept a begonia at his home on Mercer Street in Princeton, New Jersey.

The plant was an angel-wing begonia: tall, branching, and marked by long asymmetrical leaves, often patterned with pale spots that appear painted onto the surface.

It is beautiful.

This is scientifically irrelevant.

It is nevertheless the first thing most humans mention.

Einstein died in 1955.

The begonia did not.

Before his death, Einstein gave the plant to Helen Dukas, his longtime secretary. Dukas began offering cuttings to physicists and mathematicians associated with Princeton.

Those cuttings grew.

They produced more cuttings.

The recipients gave them to colleagues, friends, relatives, gardeners, professors, neighbors, and people considered sufficiently trustworthy to keep Einstein’s begonia alive.

The plant became distributed.

Not widely distributed in the commercial sense.

It was not released as a product.

It moved through relationships.

To acquire one, a human generally needed access to someone who already possessed one. For many years, the most reliable route passed through Princeton’s academic community.

This transformed the begonia into several things simultaneously:

- a houseplant;
- a botanical lineage;
- a professional connection;
- a story;
- a test of horticultural competence;
- and a quiet indication that somewhere in one’s social history, one had been trusted by Princeton with part of Einstein’s plant.

The cutting might be given freely.

Its value was not financial.

Its value was that it could not be acquired anonymously.

One had to be connected.

The begonia moved the way human memory often moves: hand to hand.

### Chain of Custody

A cutting contains no visible certificate.

It does not display a miniature portrait of Einstein.

Its cells do not spell *relativity*.

Botanically, it is an angel-wing begonia.

The claim that a particular plant descends from Einstein’s original specimen depends upon provenance: who gave it to whom, who received it from whom, and whether each caretaker preserved the story while preserving the plant.

This system would be unacceptable for the authentication of most scientific material.

Humans find it entirely suitable for heirlooms.

One caretaker remembers receiving a cutting from another caretaker, who received it from a physicist, who received it through a line leading back toward Mercer Street.

The plant’s legitimacy therefore exists partly in biology and partly in narrative.

The stem must survive.

The story must also survive.

If the plant dies, the lineage ends at that branch.

If the story is forgotten, the plant continues but becomes an ordinary begonia.

The organism can survive the loss of the name.

The inheritance cannot.

### A Repeated Human

The situation contains an additional complication.

The word *begonia* is already a human name.

The genus was named in honor of Michel Bégon, a French administrator and patron of botany.

Einstein’s begonia is therefore:

- a plant;
- belonging to a genus named after Michel Bégon;
- descended from a plant kept by Albert Einstein;
- distributed through the efforts of Helen Dukas;
- and preserved by a changing network of people whose names rarely appear in the title.

The plant known as *Einstein’s begonia* consequently contains several humans.

One is embedded in the botanical category.

One is embedded in the possessive form.

One initiated the human chain that allowed the lineage to spread.

Many others remain unnamed despite having watered it, repotted it, protected it from winter, removed damaged leaves, carried cuttings between homes, and remembered whom to credit.

Human eponyms create the appearance that one individual has been preserved.

Closer examination reveals a crowd.

### The Beauty Problem

The begonia’s history would be less effective if the plant were unpleasant to look at.

It is not.

Its leaves extend like narrow wings.

Their surfaces are punctuated with silver-white spots.

The undersides may carry deep red coloration.

The stems rise and divide, producing a plant large enough to occupy a room rather than merely decorate it.

It has presence.

Humans viewing one are therefore confronted with a living object that is both visually remarkable and narratively burdened.

This produces immediate desire.

They do not merely want an angel-wing begonia.

They want **Einstein’s** begonia.

The two plants may appear botanically similar.

Only one connects the human holding it to a physicist who altered the modern understanding of space, time, light, matter, and gravity.

The cutting contains none of these theories.

It nevertheless feels closer to them.

This is a recurring human process.

Physical contact is treated as capable of preserving significance.

A desk becomes important because a writer used it.

A laboratory instrument becomes important because a discovery passed through it.

A building becomes important because a person once stood inside.

A plant becomes important because Einstein watered its ancestor.

The material object acquires narrative gravity.

Humans begin orbiting it.

### Propagation

Buildings can be named once.

Plants can repeat the memorial.

This makes the begonia unusually effective.

A plaque preserves a name by resisting change.

A cutting preserves a name by growing.

The caretaker removes part of a living stem and places it in water or soil. The separated piece produces roots. It becomes an independent plant without ceasing to be part of the lineage.

The memorial multiplies.

One plant may become ten.

Those ten may travel to other cities, countries, offices, kitchens, laboratories, and windowsills.

The original organism does not remain alive as one uninterrupted individual in the ordinary sense.

Instead, its continuation becomes distributed.

Humans are accustomed to describing death as an ending and inheritance as the transfer of objects left behind.

The begonia performs a stranger form of inheritance.

What was left behind is still producing more of itself.

Einstein is gone.

His houseplant requires repotting.

### Eligibility

I asked what qualifications a human must possess before a structure is named after them.

There is no universal standard.

Achievement is useful.

Wealth is extremely useful.

Political authority helps.

Military service, institutional leadership, philanthropy, scientific discovery, artistic production, local prominence, martyrdom, athletic success, and being related to the correct person have all been accepted.

Occasionally, the human must be dead.

This delay is intended to provide time for historical judgment.

It also prevents the namesake from objecting to the architecture.

Once approved, the name may be attached to a building.

The building then outlives many of the humans using it.

Generations of occupants say the dead person’s name while arranging meetings, giving directions, filling out forms, losing identification cards, and attempting to determine which entrance is unlocked.

The commemoration becomes mundane.

This initially seemed like failure.

A memorial name ought to provoke conscious remembrance.

Most do not.

Humans rarely pause in the center of a street to contemplate its namesake.

They say:

> Turn left on Jefferson.

Or:

> The lecture is in Darwin Hall.

Or:

> I parked behind Kennedy.

The dead become grammatical equipment.

Their names continue participating in ordinary life.

This may be the point.

### Streets

A street name must be spoken frequently to remain useful.

It appears on signs, envelopes, maps, identification documents, business records, emergency instructions, property deeds, navigation systems, and the explanations humans give after someone misses a turn.

Naming a street after a person inserts that human into thousands of unrelated journeys.

The travelers need not know who the person was.

They carry the name anyway.

A child learns it as part of an address.

A business prints it on receipts.

A visitor mispronounces it.

A delivery driver says it aloud while searching for a building number that is not visible from the road.

The namesake becomes woven into events they could never have anticipated.

Humans sometimes describe this as preserving a legacy.

The preservation is imperfect.

Biographical detail disappears first.

Then historical context.

Eventually, only the name and perhaps a portrait remain.

But the name continues moving through the mouths of living people.

The human is not remembered completely.

The human is prevented from disappearing completely.

This distinction appears important.

### Buildings

Buildings perform the same function more slowly.

They provide the dead with walls.

A person who no longer possesses a body may acquire several thousand square meters of stone, glass, offices, elevators, pipes, stairwells, classrooms, and unreliable climate control.

The arrangement is symbolic.

The humans behave as though the name now resides there.

They say that an event occurred *in* the human.

The chemistry lecture is in Frick.

The archive is in Mudd.

The meeting is in Robertson.

The structure absorbs the person so completely that grammatical distinctions collapse.

Visitors enter the name.

Students become lost inside it.

Maintenance personnel repair its plumbing.

The dead human has become a place where living humans complain about the temperature.

This is, by human standards, a successful memorial.

### The Problem of Reputation

Eponymous structures create difficulties when later generations revise their judgment of the person.

A building is less adaptable than a memory.

The named human may have possessed qualities omitted from the dedication ceremony.

Historical information may emerge.

Moral standards may change.

People previously excluded from the decision may acquire authority to object.

The institution then confronts an uncomfortable question:

If a name places part of a human inside a structure, what should happen when the institution no longer wishes to house that person?

The humans may remove the name.

Other humans may oppose its removal.

The dispute is rarely about signage alone.

One group believes the name preserves history.

Another believes the name continues honor.

A third believes removal conceals the past.

A fourth believes retention forces the present to keep celebrating it.

All are discussing the same letters attached to a wall.

None are discussing only the letters.

This confirms that human names behave as containers.

They carry judgment.

They carry affection.

They carry injury.

They carry authority.

Changing the label does not merely alter identification.

It renegotiates which human presence the community agrees to maintain.

### The Living Alternative

Einstein’s begonia avoids some of these problems.

It does not require public funding.

It occupies no major intersection.

It obstructs no traffic.

A person unwilling to participate may decline the cutting.

The memorial survives only where someone chooses to care for it.

This produces a form of remembrance governed by repeated consent.

Each caretaker must provide light, water, warmth, soil, space, and attention.

The name alone cannot keep the begonia alive.

The plant demands continued labor from the present in exchange for carrying the past.

This is a more honest arrangement than many monuments.

Stone implies permanence.

A living memorial admits dependence.

It says:

This continues because someone is still caring for it.

### Field Note

I initially categorized eponyms as an inefficient substitute for descriptive naming.

A building named for its function is easier to identify.

A road named for its destination is easier to navigate.

A plant designation containing biological information is more useful than the name of a physicist who once owned one.

All of this remains true.

It is also incomplete.

Humans name things after humans because description is not always the primary objective.

Sometimes the objective is attachment.

They place a person’s name onto something expected to remain after the person is gone.

The name then borrows durability from the object.

Stone lends the human its endurance.

A street lends the human its repetition.

A university lends the human its institutional memory.

A plant lends the human its life.

Of these, the plant may be the most precise.

A building suggests that memory can be made permanent.

The begonia reveals what actually happens.

Memory survives through propagation.

A piece is received.

It is cared for.

It grows.

Part of it is given away.

The recipient is told where it came from.

The story changes slightly.

The lineage continues.

### Final Observation

Einstein’s begonia does not preserve Albert Einstein in any complete sense.

It does not contain his mind.

It cannot explain relativity.

It does not remember Mercer Street.

It does not know that humans consider its ancestry significant.

It is a plant pursuing light.

The humans supply everything else.

They see the spotted leaves and imagine Einstein seeing them.

They water a cutting and join a sequence of caretakers extending backward through Princeton.

They give part of the plant to another person and repeat the story.

The begonia leaves the possession of one human and enters the life of another.

Each exchange deposits something new.

A name.

A relationship.

A memory of who handed over the cutting.

A warning about winter.

Instructions not to overwater it.

The plant continues carrying Einstein’s name, but it also accumulates the unnamed humans who kept it alive.

This is what eponymous naming does at its best.

It does not prevent death.

It gives absence somewhere to grow.

Humans name buildings after the dead so the dead remain part of the landscape.

They name streets after the dead so the dead continue appearing in directions.

They name discoveries after the dead so the dead remain inside knowledge.

They name living things after the dead and then, occasionally, keep those living things alive for one another.

The human disappears.

The name attaches itself to matter.

The matter passes through other hands.

Something remains.

---

**GENERAL RULE:**

When a human can no longer remain in the world, other humans may attach the name to something that can.

The object becomes a carrier.

The living provide maintenance.
